Let's begin by discussing the distinction between Free Software and Open Source Software. While they are often combined as FOSS or FLOSS, they actually have different historical roots and philosophical approaches. The term "Free Software" with the definition of the described four freedoms goes back to Richard Stallman and the GNU project he announced in 1983—almost a decade before the emergence of Linux. In 1985, Stallman also founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF) to provide organizational, legal, and financial support for the GNU Project. The acronym GNU stands for "GNU is not Unix," which was a technically driven project to rebuild the Unix operating system from scratch to make it available to the general public. The Free Software movement is essentially a social and political movement focused on user freedom. When we talk about "free" here, we mean "free as in free speech, not free beer." It is an ethical stance advocating for user liberty against proprietary software, which treats software as property and wants to keep it under lock and key.
On the other side, the "Open Source Software" movement emerged later, taking a much more pragmatic and technical approach. As Linux gained massive popularity and the collaborative nature of the internet expanded, the ideological and social aspects began to take a back seat. For open source advocates, making the source code available is not primarily a moral crusade, but a superior, highly efficient method for developing and debugging software. By opening up the code, developers worldwide can collaborate, audit, and improve the product. When software does not grant these freedoms, it is called proprietary. This division between the ethical, social focus of Free Software and the business-oriented, pragmatic focus of Open Source has occasionally led to compatibility and licensing conflicts.
